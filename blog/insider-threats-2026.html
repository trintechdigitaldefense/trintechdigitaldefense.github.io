Insider Threats in 2026: The $19.5M Problem Trinidad Businesses Are Ignoring

Thirty percent of all confirmed data breaches in the world involve an insider. That figure comes from the 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report by Verizon, the largest breach dataset ever compiled, which analyzed 12,195 confirmed breaches across 22,052 incidents between November 2023 and October 2024. The 30 percent is not a tail risk. It is the majority pattern in many regions -- 29 percent in EMEA, where the insider threat problem has been worst documented for years, and steadily rising across other regions as remote work, cloud migration, and generative AI expand the surface area for internal compromise.

Inside Trinidad and Tobago, the pattern is visible in the same way. The Trinidad and Tobago Cyber Security Incident Response Team reported that 70 percent of all cyber incidents in 2025 were phishing and business email compromise -- attacks that succeed not by exploiting infrastructure, but by compromising the people who manage it. The local banking community lost over TT$3 million to cyber-enabled fraud in a single fiscal year, according to the Trinidad and Tobago Police Service Intelligence and Investigation unit. At its core, as the TTPS noted, cybercrime is profoundly human. The attackers do not hack firewalls. They hack people -- through phishing credentials, through social engineering, through exploiting the legitimate access that every employee already carries.

The global data puts a dollar figure on what "hacking people" costs. The Ponemon Institute priced the average annual cost of insider risk at US$19.5 million per organization in its 2026 Cost of Insider Risks Global Report, up 12 percent year over year from US$17.4 million in the 2025 edition, across a survey of 1,176 firms. IBM's 2025 Cost of a Data Breach Report named malicious insiders the most expensive attack vector overall at US$4.92 million per incident -- more than half a million dollars above the global average of US$4.44 million. On a per-incident basis, credential theft costs US$779,707, negligent insiders cost US$676,517, and malicious insiders cost US$715,366.

The average organization spent nearly $20 million in 2026 dealing with insider threats. This article explains how insider threats work, what the data shows about their scale and cost, how the problem is accelerating with generative AI and remote work, and what Trinidad businesses can do to protect themselves before an insider causes a breach.

What Is an Insider Threat?

An insider threat is any security risk that originates from within an organization. The term covers three distinct categories, each with different motivations and detection challenges:

Negligent insiders are employees who cause harm through carelessness rather than malice. They send sensitive files to the wrong recipient. They misconfigure cloud storage so that customer databases are publicly accessible. They paste confidential data into a generative AI tool without thinking about where that data goes. They fall for a phishing email and hand over credentials. Negligent insiders drive the majority of insider incidents -- 55 percent according to Ponemon's 2025 report -- but each individual event tends to be less costly than a malicious or credential-based breach.

Malicious insiders are employees who deliberately abuse their access to steal data, sabotage systems, or facilitate external attacks. The departing-employee data theft scenario is the canonical example: an employee preparing to leave for a competitor systematically downloads customer lists, financial models, and source code over weeks before resigning. The 2023 Tesla insider case -- where two former employees leaked over 23,000 internal documents containing 100GB of confidential data including personal information for 75,000+ employees -- is the most widely cited case. A malicious insider carries the highest per-event cost because they have legitimate access that is harder to detect, they often target regulated or high-value data, and the reputational damage is concentrated.

Credential theft occupies a middle ground. The person whose credentials are stolen is not an insider in the malicious sense, but the attacker uses insider-accessible credentials to cause damage. The 2024 Snowflake campaign tracked by Mandiant -- where 165 customer organizations were affected because the attacker used credentials exposed by infostealers, some dating back to 2020 -- is a prime example. The "insider" wasn't a Snowflake employee. The attacker used stolen insider credentials to cause an insider-scale breach.

The distinction matters because each category requires a different defense. Negligent insiders need user behavior monitoring and process controls. Malicious insiders need privileged access management and departure monitoring. Credential theft needs MFA, key rotation, and infostealer detection. Treating all three as the same problem -- the way many organizations do by relying solely on awareness training -- leaves two of them largely unaddressed.

How Big Is the Problem in 2026?

The data from multiple sources converges on a single conclusion: insider threats are at a recorded high and growing across every dimension.

Annual cost per organization. The Ponemon Institute found that the average annual cost of insider risk rose from US$8.3 million in 2018 to US$19.5 million in 2026, a 109 percent increase. The 2026 figure represents a 12 percent year-over-year jump. The cost is not spread evenly across categories: negligent insider costs rose to US$10.3 million (up 17 percent), malicious insider costs reached US$4.7 million (up 27 percent), and credential theft costs were US$4.5 million.

Share of breaches. Verizon's 2025 DBIR found that 30 percent of breaches involved insiders globally. The share varied by region -- 29 percent in EMEA, 5 percent in North America, and 1 percent in APAC -- but the global average masks the fact that the problem is regionally concentrated and growing. The Privilege Misuse pattern alone, the closest direct mapping to intentional malicious insider activity, accounted for 6 percent of all breaches. Within insider breaches specifically, 8 percent involved misuse, 19 percent were unintentional mistakes, and the remainder were misdelivery, misconfiguration, and publishing errors.

Cost per incident. IBM's 2025 report found that malicious insiders cause breaches costing an average of US$4.92 million, the highest of any attack vector. Phishing follows at US$4.8 million. The malicious insider cost is higher than the global average of US$4.44 million because insider breaches tend to involve more sensitive data (intellectual property, regulated data), take longer to detect (the attacker already has legitimate access), and require more complex containment (forensic preservation, HR coordination, legal action).

Detection speed. Ponemon found the mean time to contain an insider incident was 81 days in 2025, down from 86 days in 2023 but still three times longer than most external breach detection timelines. Containment time is the single biggest cost lever: incidents contained in under 30 days cost US$14.2 million annually, while those exceeding 90 days cost US$21.9 million. A 60-day reduction in containment time is worth roughly US$8 million to the average organization.

Remote work and AI acceleration. Two forces are amplifying the insider threat problem. Remote and hybrid work, cited by 75 percent of security leaders as the primary insider risk driver, increases the attack surface by moving sensitive data across personal devices and home networks. Generative AI is creating a new class of unintentional insider: Cyberhaven measured a 30x increase in data sent to generative AI apps in a single year, with 22 percent of files and 4.37 percent of prompts containing sensitive corporate information. Seventy-seven percent of employees share sensitive data via ChatGPT or peer AI tools. Copy-paste into GenAI is now the single biggest vector for corporate data leaving enterprise control.

The DPRK IT Worker Scheme: When "Insider" Meets "Nation State"

The single biggest insider story of 2024 and 2025 is the North Korean IT worker infiltration scheme, and it illustrates how insider threats blur the line between employee and external attacker.

Skilled North Korean IT workers operating from China, Russia, and other locations apply for fully remote positions at Western companies using stolen identities, AI-altered photos, and deepfake-augmented video interviews. Once hired, they receive corporate laptops shipped to intermediaries in the United States ("laptop farms") and work from overseas through remote-management software. They draw salaries, send most of the money back to Pyongyang to fund the regime's weapons programs, and frequently exfiltrate intellectual property or attempt extortion before disappearing.

The scale is substantial. CrowdStrike tracked a 220 percent year-over-year increase in DPRK-nexus IT worker infiltration, with 320-plus companies infiltrated in the last 12 months to mid-2025. Multiple vendors track the same activity under different names: FAMOUS CHOLLIMA (CrowdStrike), UNC5267 (Mandiant), Jasper Sleet (Microsoft), and Wagemole (various). Mandiant reported that fraudulent North Korean IT workers accounted for 5 percent of all investigated incidents in 2024.

The U.S. government estimated that DPRK IT workers generated nearly US$800 million in 2024. The Department of Justice conducted a coordinated enforcement sweep on June 30, 2025, searching 29 laptop farms across 16 states. Christina Chapman, an Arizona woman who operated one of the largest laptop farms, was sentenced in July 2025 to 102 months in federal prison for a scheme that generated over US$17 million and impacted 300-plus U.S. companies.

The case that exposed the pattern to the public was KnowBe4's July 2024 disclosure. A candidate passed a multi-stage interview process including background checks, verified references, and four video-conference interviews -- all using a stolen U.S.-based identity and an AI-augmented stock photo. KnowBe4 shipped a corporate laptop. Within hours, the device began loading malware. KnowBe4's EDR caught the activity and contained the incident within 25 minutes of the first alert.

For Trinidad and Tobago businesses that hire remote talent, outsource development, or engage with international contractors, the DPRK IT worker scheme is not a U.S.-only problem. The same tactics are used globally. Any organization that hires remote engineering, IT, or support staff through job boards and video interviews without verifying the physical presence and identity of the candidate is exposed to the same attack pattern.

How Insider Attacks Reach Trinidad Businesses

The attack chain that leads to insider-driven breaches follows predictable stages, and each stage is relevant to Trinidad's own incident patterns.

Stage one: credential acquisition. The TT-CSIRT data showing 70 percent of Trinidad's incidents as phishing and BEC is the credential acquisition phase of the insider attack chain. A finance employee at a Trinidad business clicks a phishing link and enters their credentials. The attacker now has an insider identity -- not a malicious insider by motivation, but a compromised insider by capability. This credential then enables every downstream attack: accessing customer databases, modifying banking details, initiating fraudulent transfers, exfiltrating data.

Stage two: privilege mapping. Once inside, the attacker -- whether a malicious insider or an external attacker using stolen credentials -- explores what they can reach. They check what permissions their account provides. They look for overprivileged colleagues, for cloud storage buckets, for financial systems they can access without further authentication. At a typical Trinidad business with a handful of IT staff and minimal privileged access management, the answer to "what can this account reach" is often "everything."

Stage three: data access and exfiltration. The attacker reaches their objective. In Trinidad's case, the data from the TTPS shows the objective is financial -- redirecting payments, draining accounts, manipulating vendor details. The TT$3 million lost by the local banking community represents the aggregate outcome of this stage. For non-financial businesses, the objective may be customer data, intellectual property, or leverage for ransom.

Stage four: evasion and persistence. Malicious insiders and compromised credentials are particularly dangerous because the attacker operates with legitimate access. They do not trigger the same alerts as external attackers. They access resources during normal hours. They use approved tools and channels. They blend into the background of normal business activity. Mandiant measured a median dwell time of 11 days across all attack types in 2024, but insider-specific dwell is often longer because the activity looks like legitimate work until it does not.

Generative AI: The New Insider Accelerator

Generative AI is creating a new category of insider threat that existing security programs are not equipped to handle: the unintentional data exfiltrator who is copying sensitive information into public AI tools because it is convenient.

Cyberhaven's research measured a 30x increase in data sent to generative AI apps in a single year. The average organization shares more than 7.7 gigabytes of data with AI tools per month. Twenty-two percent of files uploaded to GenAI contain sensitive corporate information -- source code, access credentials, M&A documents, customer records, internal financial data. Among users who paste to GenAI tools, the average is 6.8 pastes per day, of which more than half (3.8 pastes) include sensitive corporate data. Copy-paste into GenAI is now ranked the single biggest vector for corporate data leaving enterprise control.

IBM's 2025 Cost of a Data Breach Report priced shadow AI at US$670,000 in additional cost per incident. Sixty-three percent of researched organizations had no AI governance policies in place. Gurucul's 2026 Insider Risk Report found that 94 percent of organizations said AI is increasing their exposure to insider threats, and 90 percent experienced at least one insider incident in the past 12 months, with more than half costing US$500,000+ to remediate.

For Trinidad businesses, the AI insider threat is particularly insidious because the risk comes from well-meaning employees, not malicious actors. An employee pastes a customer spreadsheet into ChatGPT to ask it to format the data. They paste internal financial projections into an AI tool to ask it to generate a summary. They paste source code into a code-generation AI to speed up development. In each case, the data leaves the organization's control and enters a third-party system where it may be stored, used for model training, or exposed through logs and APIs. The employee did not intend to compromise security. The result is the same.

What Makes Insider Threats Harder to Detect

Insider threats succeed where external attacks fail because they do not face the same barriers. An external attacker has to get through firewalls, gain credentials, avoid detection tools, and move laterally through a network they do not understand. An insider -- or someone using insider credentials -- already has everything an external attacker needs: legitimate access, knowledge of where the data lives, and the ability to operate without triggering the same suspicion.

The detection challenge is compounded by several factors:

  • Legitimate access masks malicious activity. When an employee with admin access downloads a customer database, it looks like normal work. When an external attacker does it, it looks like a breach. The same action is invisible when the actor has a legitimate reason to do it.
  • Departure spikes are predictable but rarely monitored. Cyberhaven's longitudinal research found that employees are 83.1 percent more likely to exfiltrate data in the two weeks before resigning and 109.3 percent more likely on the day of termination. Yet most organizations do not tag resignation notices in their HR systems and monitor the trailing data activity.
  • Privileged access is rarely audited. DTEX's 2026 edition found that Privileged Access Management (PAM) delivers US$6.1 million in annual savings -- the single highest-ROI control by a wide margin. Yet most organizations still grant standing admin access that is never reviewed. User and Entity Behavior Analytics (UEBA) delivers US$5.1 million in savings, but is deployed far less frequently than user training, which delivers only US$4.8 million and is the most widely deployed control at 83 percent adoption.
  • The CISO-board perception gap. EY's 2025 Cybersecurity Study found that 47 percent of CISOs say their organization has experienced an insider-driven incident in the past three years, compared with 31 percent of the rest of the C-suite. The operational reality is invisible to boards and executives who do not see the incident counts. This perception gap means insider risk rarely gets the budget or attention it needs.

What Trinidad Businesses Should Do

Protecting against insider threats is not about treating every employee as a potential adversary. It is about implementing controls that detect and prevent the specific patterns that insider attacks follow, regardless of intent.

Implement privileged access management. This is the single highest-ROI control for insider risk at US$6.1 million in annual savings. Anyone with admin, root, domain controller, cloud-IAM-admin, or git-write-to-protected-branch privileges should be on a just-in-time access workflow with session recording. Standing admin access is the primary enabler for both malicious and negligent insider incidents. Remove standing privileges. Require approval for elevated access. Record sessions. Rotate keys quarterly.

Deploy user and entity behavior analytics. UEBA delivers US$5.1 million in annual savings by detecting anomalous patterns: unusual data downloads, off-hours access, mass file transfers to personal cloud storage, access to systems the user has never touched before. The key is establishing a behavioral baseline for each user and alerting when activity deviates from that baseline.

Monitor the 30-day departure window. When an employee gives notice of resignation or is terminated, the risk of data exfiltration spikes. Tag every notice-of-resignation in HRIS. Lock down outbound file transfers to personal cloud storage and personal email for that user. Require SOC review of any sensitive-data download in the trailing 30 days. Monitor for increased use of USB drives, personal email, and cloud storage services. This single control catches a high percentage of departing-employee data theft before it happens.

Enforce multi-factor authentication everywhere. Credential theft is the highest per-event-cost insider category at US$779,707. MFA is the most effective control against credential compromise. Microsoft found that MFA stops more than 99 percent of identity-based attacks. Enforce MFA on every account that supports it -- email, cloud platforms, financial systems, VPN, remote access. Where possible, use phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2 security keys or certificate-based authentication), which stops the credential theft vector entirely.

Govern generative AI use. Push official, organization-controlled AI tools. Block unsanctioned GenAI access at the network proxy. Deploy data loss prevention that inspects GenAI prompts for sensitive data classes. Run targeted user training that names the specific types of data employees should never paste into AI tools. IBM's US$670,000 shadow-AI cost premium is the cost of not having a policy; the cost of implementing one is a fraction of that.

Validate remote hires. For any organization that hires remote talent or engages contractors, implement identity verification beyond resume and video interviews. Require video-on interviews. Verify identity documents. Check for inconsistencies in work history, references, and time zones. Monitor for impossible-travel patterns in the first 30 days post-onboarding. Install endpoint detection and response from Day 1. The KnowBe4 case demonstrates that even companies with robust hiring processes can be compromised if they do not verify the physical identity of the candidate.

Conduct insider-focused penetration tests. The gap between having a control and that control catching a determined insider is wide. Internal penetration tests that start from an assumed-internal foothold, assumed-breach engagements that test what a compromised insider can reach, and red team scenarios that exercise departing-employee and privilege-abuse paths are the only way to know your detection actually fires before a multi-million-dollar incident does the validation for you.

The Compliance Angle

For Trinidad businesses in regulated sectors -- banks, insurance companies, financial services providers, healthcare organizations -- insider threats are not a technical problem. They are a compliance requirement.

SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001, and PCI DSS all require documented access controls, periodic access reviews, privilege management, and evidence of least-privilege enforcement. A malicious insider who exfiltrates customer data because standing admin access was never reviewed is a direct compliance violation. IBM found that implementing privileged access management lowered the average breach cost by US$225,622, and offensive security testing lowered it by US$211,339. Both are factors that appear in audit evidence and executive testimony.

The T&T regulatory landscape adds additional pressure. The CARICOM Cyber Security and Cybercrime Action Plan (CCSCAP), launched in Port-of-Spain in October 2025, establishes the framework for harmonized cybercrime legislation and incident response. The Telecommunications Authority's 2025-2028 Strategic Plan embeds cybersecurity priorities into national infrastructure regulations. Organizations that fail to demonstrate proper insider risk controls -- particularly privileged access management and departure monitoring -- may find themselves non-compliant with these evolving frameworks.

The Bottom Line

Insider threats are not a theoretical problem for Trinidad and Tobago businesses. They are active, documented, and accelerating. The data is clear: 30 percent of confirmed breaches involve insiders. Malicious insiders cause the most expensive breaches at $4.92 million each. The annual cost to organizations has climbed to $19.5 million and is growing 12 percent per year. Remote work and generative AI are expanding the attack surface, and the detection gap remains wide at 81 days median containment time.

Trinidad's own incident data -- 70 percent of incidents being phishing and BEC, TT$3 million lost to cyber fraud -- confirms that the primary attack vector is human, not infrastructure. The attackers who compromised Trinidad businesses in 2025 did not need to exploit zero-day vulnerabilities. They needed a credentialed person to make a mistake.

The controls are well-understood. Privileged access management is the highest-ROI control at $6.1 million in annual savings. User behavior analytics adds $5.1 million. MFA stops 99 percent of credential attacks. Departure monitoring catches the pre-resignation exfiltration spike. GenAI governance prevents the new class of unintentional insider. Each of these controls addresses a specific insider attack pattern. Organizations that implement all of them together create a defense that catches both malicious and negligent insiders, reduces breach costs by millions of dollars, and satisfies compliance requirements.

For organizations in Trinidad that do not have dedicated insider risk programs, which is the majority, the choice is not between building a full insider risk program or accepting the risk.
