Password Security in 2026: Why Your Employees' Passwords Are Still 123456

6 billion passwords were stolen by malware in 2025. In 2025, more than 97 percent of identity attacks Microsoft saw were password attacks, and identity-based attacks rose 32 percent in the first half of that year alone. 65 percent of exposed consumer accounts and 42 percent of exposed corporate accounts reuse at least one password across multiple services. In NordPass's 2025 ranking of the most common passwords, 123456 ranked first again. The same list included admin, 12345678, 123456789 and 12345. Password security has been a known problem for decades, and the data from 2025 and 2026 shows it is getting worse, not better.

The scale of credential theft has outpaced every security initiative organisations have deployed to address it. Specops, which tracks millions of breached passwords annually, found that about 230 million of the 1 billion-plus passwords stolen by malware in 2025 still met standard complexity requirements. Users met the rules in predictable ways: capitalising the first letter, appending 1 or the current year, swapping a for @. The result is a credential ecosystem where billions of stolen passwords circulate on dark web markets and combo list databases, where attackers programmatically test them against every login page they can find, and where a password that technically meets your organisation's complexity policy is statistically indistinguishable from 123456 to a cracking tool.

Inside Trinidad and Tobago, this threat is amplified by the same factors that make credential attacks dangerous everywhere: widespread password reuse, incomplete MFA adoption, and the steady growth of credential theft via infostealer malware. The Trinidad and Tobago Cyber Security Incident Response Team reported that 70 percent of all cyber incidents in 2025 were phishing and business email compromise, both of which end in credential theft. Every phishing click is a potential infostealer delivery vector. Every infostealer infection produces hundreds of stolen credentials. Every reused password connects one compromised account to dozens of others across work and personal services.

This article explains what the data shows about password security in 2026, how attackers exploit password weaknesses at scale, and what organisations can do to protect themselves beyond the familiar advice to use stronger passwords, which has not worked in 30 years.

What the Data Shows About Password Habits in 2026

The global picture of password security in 2026 is defined by three realities: people still choose weak passwords, they still reuse them across services, and attackers have tools that make both of those choices devastating.

Password reuse is the dominant risk. SpyCloud's 2026 Identity Exposure Report measured something different from surveys. Rather than asking people what they do, it measured what attackers actually see in compromised data. Of the exposed consumer accounts in its database, 65 percent reused at least one password across multiple services. Of exposed corporate accounts, 42 percent reused passwords. This is not a self-reported figure. It is measured from actual credential dumps collected from infostealer infections, breach datasets, and public data stores. The domino effect is real: an attacker who compromises one employee's personal email account through a reused password can try that same credential on the employee's corporate email, their VPN, their cloud workspace, and every service they access at work. Password reuse is how a low-value compromise becomes a high-value breach.

Complexity rules do not prevent weak passwords. The most common passwords in 2025 were the same ones from every previous year: 123456, 123456789, 12345678, 12345, password, admin. These entries are simple numeric sequences and dictionary words that fall to automated cracking tools in fractions of a second. They also appear in the top 10 of NordPass's annual rankings year after year. Specops's 2026 Breached Password Report, based on more than 6 billion passwords stolen in 2025, found the same leaders: 123456, 123456789 and 12345678 topped the list. These are not edge cases. These are the default choices for a large share of the global population, and they appear in every breached dataset.

Complexity rules create the illusion of security. Users meet complexity rules in extremely predictable ways. Capitalise the first letter. Append 1 or the current year. Use ! as the sole symbol. The string Password1! or admin123 or pass@123 may satisfy a policy that requires one uppercase letter, one number, and one special character, but to an attacker's cracking tool, these are the first entries tried after the plain dictionary word. Hashcat includes rule sets specifically designed to apply these patterns automatically. The result is passwords that look secure on paper and in compliance checklists but are trivially weak in practice. This phenomenon is so well documented that NIST has explicitly removed mandatory complexity rules from its latest guidelines, replacing them with length requirements and banned password checks against known breach data.

Infostealer malware accelerates credential theft. In 2025, SpyCloud recaptured 642.4 million credentials from 13.2 million infostealer infections, an average of about 50 credentials per infection. Those credentials include saved browser passwords, session cookies, form autofill data, and system information. A single infected employee laptop produces a log containing passwords for work services, personal email, banking, healthcare portals, and every other service where the user had saved a login. In 2024, the equivalent figure was 548 million credentials from 18 million infections, meaning the per-infection yield has grown and the total credential volume from malware has continued to increase.

How Attackers Exploit Password Weaknesses

Attackers do not need to invent new techniques to exploit password weaknesses. The attack patterns are well documented, consistently effective, and executed at massive scale.

Credential stuffing. Attackers take credentials from one breach and try them against login pages for unrelated services. If an employee reuses their personal email password on a work account, and that personal password was leaked in a breach, the attacker can log in to the work account. Akamai recorded 193 billion credential stuffing attacks globally in 2020, and this pattern has not decreased. Microsoft reports that more than 97 percent of identity attacks it sees are password attacks, and identity-based attacks rose 32 percent in the first half of 2025. Credential stuffing targets websites, APIs, and mobile login endpoints, scaling automatically against every login page on the internet.

Password spraying. Instead of trying many passwords against one account, attackers try a few common passwords against thousands of accounts. Winter2025! is tried across every email address in an organisation's directory. If any account uses that password -- a common choice when organisations set it as a temporary password for onboarding -- the attacker gains access without triggering account lockouts, because only one account fails at a time. This technique is especially effective against organisations that rely on password-only authentication for services like VPNs or remote desktop.

Infostealer log exploitation. Infostealer malware extracts saved passwords from browsers, email clients, and applications. These logs are sold on criminal markets in bulk. Brokers filter the logs by domain, extracting all passwords for gmail.com, outlook.com, or a specific corporate domain. The filtered credentials are then tested against live authentication endpoints, either by the seller or by a separate verification service. Verified credentials are sold at a premium to buyers who target specific industries or organisations.

Third-party credential compromise. Attackers breach a small vendor, a marketing firm, or a supply chain partner, extract employee credentials, and then try those credentials at the larger target organisation. Employees commonly reuse personal or minor-site passwords for work accounts, creating a bridge from the breached vendor to the target. In 2024, Verizon found that 54 percent of ransomware victims had their domains appear in infostealer logs or marketplace postings, and 40 percent had corporate email addresses among the compromised credentials. This suggests a supply chain where attackers buy stealer logs and use those credentials to break into organisations, often via VPN or RDP.

MFA bypass techniques. Even when MFA is enabled, attackers have developed methods that bypass it. MFA fatigue (push bombing) sends hundreds of authentication requests until the user approves one out of frustration. Adversary-in-the-middle (AiTM) phishing proxies capture not just the password and the one-time code, but the session cookie, which remains valid for hours or days after the phishing attack. Microsoft reported AiTM phishing attacks up 146 percent in a year, and token theft incidents at about 39,000 per day. A session cookie bypasses MFA entirely because the authentication already happened on the victim's machine.

The Gap Between MFA Adoption and MFA Effectiveness

MFA is one of the most effective security controls available, and adoption is incomplete. Okta's Secure Sign-in Trends Report 2025 found that 70 percent of workforce users at Okta customers were enrolled in MFA as of January 2025, up from 66 percent a year earlier. That still leaves nearly 30 percent of users without MFA. In Microsoft's data, only 41 percent of Entra ID monthly active users at enterprise customers signed in with MFA in 2024, even though a Microsoft study found MFA lowers the risk of account compromise by 99.22 percent. The gap between the most effective control and the largest remaining gap in security is 29 percent of the workforce.

The industry breakdown reveals uneven protection. Technology leads at 87 percent adoption. Retail sits at 52 percent. Transportation and warehousing trails at 42 percent. These are not abstract categories. They describe the sectors that are most exposed to credential attacks: retail with customer accounts storing payment information, transportation with access to logistics and inventory systems, and any business that relies on cloud-based services where the login is the only barrier to data access.

Of the users who do have MFA enabled, 14 percent use phishing-resistant authenticators, up from 8.6 percent a year earlier. This is progress, but it means that 86 percent of MFA-protected users still rely on phishable factors: SMS codes, push notifications, or authenticator app entries that can be intercepted by AiTM phishing proxies. The 63 percent year-over-year growth in phishing-resistant authenticator adoption is significant, but the base is small. For most organisations, the MFA that is currently protecting their accounts is vulnerable to the same bypass techniques that are growing in frequency.

The Cost of Password Insecurity

The financial impact of weak password security is measurable, severe, and growing.

Breaches involving compromised credentials took 292 days to identify and contain in IBM's 2024 Cost of a Data Breach report -- the longest of any vector that year. In 2025, that fell to 246 days, behind supply chain, malicious insider, and phishing breaches, but still above the 241-day global mean. Breaches starting with compromised credentials averaged $4.67 million in 2025, above the $4.44 million global average. In 2026, IBM reported a record global average breach cost of $4.99 million, with $11.5 million in the U.S. $6.64 million in healthcare, and about $6.3 million in financial services. Credential-based breaches are among the most expensive because they tend to go undetected for longer, giving attackers more time to move laterally, escalate privileges, and exfiltrate data before the breach is discovered.

The operational cost of passwords is also significant. Password resets remain a steady drain on IT help desks. In the FIDO Alliance's State of Passkeys 2026 survey, 35 percent of organisations that deployed passkeys reported fewer password reset tickets and 45 percent reported faster employee logins. For every reset ticket, there is lost productivity for the affected employee, potential security risk from weak temporary passwords, and the administrative overhead of tracking and auditing reset events.

Passkey adoption offers a path forward. The FIDO Alliance estimated 5 billion passkeys in use in May 2026 and 7 billion by October 2026, with passkeys now carrying 36 percent of successful sign-ins in its Passkey Index 2026, up from 26 percent in 2025. However, in the workforce, only 28 percent of organisations surveyed by FIDO have achieved fully passwordless authentication. Consumer services are ahead of enterprise. Most organisations are still managing passwords for thousands of accounts, still enforcing complexity rules that users bypass, still losing time to help desk resets, and still assuming that the password is the first and strongest barrier to account access.

What This Means for Trinidad and Tobago Businesses

Trinidad's businesses face specific risks in the password security landscape that amplify the global trends.

Finance and banking exposure. Trinidad's financial services sector processes transactions through multiple systems -- online banking, mobile apps, corporate banking portals, payment gateways, and internal systems that staff access via VPN. Each of these systems requires authentication. If a customer or employee reuses a password from a breached service, attackers can attempt login to any of these systems. The average breach cost in financial services is $6.3 million (IBM, 2026), and the Trinidad and Tobago Financial Services Commission expects financial institutions to maintain strong authentication controls. Password-only access to banking systems or customer data is increasingly non-compliant with regulatory expectations, not just bad practice.

Government and public sector. Government agencies in Trinidad manage sensitive citizen data, public service portals, and internal communications systems, many of which are accessed via VPN or remote desktop. These systems are targeted by attackers who use credential stuffing and password spraying to gain initial access. Once inside, the impact is not just financial -- it is operational disruption, citizen data exposure, and loss of public trust. Password policies for government systems need to be enforced with MFA and, where possible, passkeys. The NIST guidance that discourages forced password rotation is particularly relevant here, as government systems have historically relied on mandatory periodic changes that drive users to write passwords down or cycle predictable variations.

Small and medium enterprises. Trinidad's SME sector is the backbone of the local economy, and these businesses are often the weakest link in password security. Many rely on a small IT team or external support provider that manages accounts, resets passwords, and maintains server infrastructure. These teams are not equipped to implement advanced authentication controls or monitor for compromised credentials. Passwords are the default authentication method, and password reuse is widespread among employees who manage both personal and business accounts on the same devices. This combination of weak controls and high exposure makes SMEs attractive targets for credential-based attacks.

Cloud migration and third-party services. Trinidad businesses are rapidly adopting cloud services -- Microsoft 365 for email and collaboration, cloud-based ERP and accounting platforms, customer relationship management systems, payment processing APIs. Each of these services adds login endpoints that can be targeted by credential stuffing, password spraying, or AiTM phishing. Third-party integrations create additional attack surfaces: if a customer data platform or marketing tool is breached, employee credentials stored there may be reused at other services. Only 23 percent of third parties fully remediated missing or weak MFA on cloud accounts, according to the 2026 DBIR, meaning most organisations' supply chains include vendors with weak authentication.

What Businesses Should Do

Password security has not improved because the solutions focus on what users should do, not on changing the system so that weak passwords are ineffective. The following steps address the actual mechanisms through which password weaknesses lead to breaches.

Enforce phishing-resistant MFA for all workforce accounts. A Microsoft study found MFA lowers the risk of account compromise by 99.22 percent overall and by 98.56 percent for accounts whose credentials had already leaked. But MFA effectiveness depends on the factor used. Phishing-resistant authenticators -- FIDO2 security keys, Windows Hello, device-bound certificates -- block AiTM phishing, token theft, and MFA fatigue. SMS codes and push notifications are vulnerable to the bypass techniques that have grown 146 percent in a year. For workforce accounts, phishing-resistant MFA is not optional. For administrative and privileged accounts, it is mandatory. The 86 percent of MFA-protected users currently relying on phishable factors are only partially protected.

Deploy breached password checking. When users set or change a password, check it against known breach databases such as those maintained by Have I Been Pwned or SpyCloud. This prevents users from selecting passwords that have already been exposed in prior breaches, regardless of whether the password meets complexity requirements. NIST SP 800-63B-4 explicitly recommends this approach, and it is more effective than complexity rules because it targets the actual passwords attackers use, not theoretical patterns.

Eliminate forced password rotation. NIST SP 800-63B-4 has removed mandatory periodic password changes from its guidelines. Forcing users to change passwords every 60 or 90 days drives users to choose weaker passwords, reuse patterns, and write passwords down. The evidence shows that forced rotation does not improve security and actively degrades it. Instead, rotate passwords only when there is evidence of compromise. Monitor for compromised credentials through dark web monitoring and credential exposure services, and reset passwords when an alert fires. This is the approach that matches the threat model.

Monitor for credential exposure continuously. SpyCloud's database of recovered identity records grew 22 percent to 53.3 billion records in its 2025 report and another 23 percent to 65.7 billion in 2026. That is roughly eight records for every person on the planet. A monitoring service that checks sources weekly and sends a summary report at the end of the month is incompatible with a threat model where credentials can be bought, validated, and used within hours. Continuous monitoring with immediate alerting, combined with a defined response process for credential resets and session revocation, is the only approach that reduces the window between exposure and exploitation.

Inventory and rotate non-human identities. API keys, service tokens, and machine credentials have no MFA, are long-lived, and carry broad permissions. In 2025, SpyCloud counted 18.1 million exposed API keys and machine credentials. These break every mitigation built for human accounts. Inventory every service account, document who owns each key, scope it to the minimum permissions required, and rotate it on exposure. Treat API keys the same way you treat workforce passwords.

Migrate to passkeys where feasible. Passkeys represent the future of passwordless authentication. 36 percent of successful sign-ins in the FIDO Passkey Index 2026 now use passkeys, up from 26 percent in 2025. For workforce accounts, passkeys eliminate the password as an attack surface entirely. They are phishing-resistant by design, require no memory from the user, and do not generate reset tickets for the help desk. For customer-facing services, passkey adoption is growing but remains optional, meaning customers who have not enabled passkeys are still vulnerable to password reuse and credential stuffing. Organisations should prioritise passkey rollout for internal accounts first, where the organisation can control authentication, then evaluate customer-facing passkey support based on risk and user base size.

The Bottom Line

Password security is not a problem that will be solved by better awareness training or stronger complexity rules. The data from 2025 and 2026 is clear: 6 billion passwords were stolen in a single year. 65 percent of exposed consumer accounts and 42 percent of exposed corporate accounts reuse passwords. The most common password is still 123456. Credential abuse appeared at some stage in 39 percent of breaches in the 2026 DBIR. MFA adoption is stuck at 70 percent. Of the MFA that is deployed, 86 percent uses phishable factors. Breaches starting with compromised credentials average $4.67 million and take 246 days to contain.

Trinidad and Tobago businesses face these same risks, amplified by the factors that make credential attacks effective everywhere: cloud migration expanding login endpoints, third-party integrations creating supply chain attack paths, and small IT teams managing authentication without advanced controls. The solution is not better passwords. It is authentication that does not depend on passwords: phishing-resistant MFA, breached password checking, continuous credential monitoring, and a migration to passkeys for internal accounts.

Until passkeys replace passwords for the workforce -- and at the current rate of adoption, that transition will take years -- organisations must operate under the assumption that many of their user passwords are already known to attackers. Every control in the security architecture needs to be evaluated against that assumption: if the password is compromised, what stops the attacker?

Key takeaway: 6 billion passwords were stolen in 2025, and credential abuse appeared in 39 percent of breaches according to the 2026 DBIR. Password reuse connects compromised personal accounts to corporate systems. Phishing-resistant MFA reduces compromise risk by 99.22 percent. The most effective defence is not stronger passwords but authentication that does not depend on them: phishing-resistant MFA, breached password checking, continuous credential monitoring, and passkey migration.
