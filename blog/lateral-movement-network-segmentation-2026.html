Lateral Movement Attacks: Why Network Segmentation Is Trinidad's Missing Security Control

A single compromised host in a typical enterprise environment can reach 85 percent of all internal systems on its first hop and effectively 100 percent on its second. That is not an edge case. It is the current baseline, measured by Zero Networks across 312 enterprise environments and 54 trillion network activities in 2026.

87 percent of enterprise servers accept inbound RDP or SSH from broad internal sources. 80 percent of servers are reachable from anywhere inside the network. 43.2 percent of internal authentication traffic still runs on NTLM, the legacy protocol that an attacker can exploit to authenticate to other systems using only a hashed credential -- no plaintext password required. 78.7 percent of servers are reachable over SMB or WinRM. Over 70 percent of all enterprise threat activity flows through the same administrative protocols -- SMB, RDP, WinRM, RPC -- that IT departments rely on to keep the business running.

This is the architecture of lateral movement. Once an attacker gets inside -- through a phishing email, a compromised credential, a vulnerable internet-facing server -- these networks hand them a map of every system they can reach, every credential they can reuse, and every escalation path they can exploit. The attacker does not need to breach the firewall again. They do not need to break into a second account. They simply walk through the open doors that the network itself provides.

Inside Trinidad and Tobago, the risk is amplified by the same factors that make lateral movement dangerous everywhere: flat network architectures, excessive privileged access, and minimal east-west visibility. The Trinidad and Tobago Cyber Security Incident Response Team reported that 70 percent of all cyber incidents in 2025 were phishing and business email compromise. Each successful phishing attack is a potential foothold. Each compromised credential is a potential lateral movement vector. And without network segmentation, every compromised account is a key to everything inside the network.

This article explains what lateral movement is, how it works, why traditional security controls fail to stop it, and what network segmentation can do to prevent it -- before a single phishing click turns into a full enterprise compromise.

What Is Lateral Movement?

Lateral movement is the process attackers use to move from an initially compromised asset -- a laptop, a server, a cloud instance -- to additional systems, accounts, data stores, or control-plane infrastructure inside the network. In operational terms, lateral movement is how attackers convert a single compromise into enterprise-wide impact. Before lateral movement, you may have an intrusion. After lateral movement, you have downtime, ransomware propagation, data exposure, failed recovery, and executives explaining why one compromised endpoint could reach systems it never should have touched.

The typical lateral movement chain follows a predictable pattern:

Initial foothold. An attacker gains access through a compromised credential -- usually via phishing, which accounts for 70 percent of Trinidad's incidents according to TT-CSIRT. The compromised account has legitimate access to the network. It can reach mail servers, file shares, and internal applications. It can authenticate to other systems.

Reconnaissance. The attacker uses built-in tools -- Active Directory queries, network scanning, credential dumping -- to discover what systems the compromised account can reach. Tools like BloodHound, Mimikatz, and PowerShell remapping automate this process, identifying high-value targets, privilege paths, and trust relationships that the attacker can exploit.

Credential harvesting and reuse. The attacker extracts passwords, hashes, and Kerberos tickets from the initial system. NTLM hashes can be passed directly to other systems without ever recovering the plaintext password. Kerberos tickets can be used to authenticate to any service that accepts Kerberos. If the compromised account has any level of administrative access, the attacker can escalate to domain admin within minutes.

Persistence and escalation. Once the attacker reaches high-value systems -- domain controllers, file servers, ERP databases, backup systems -- they establish persistence. They install backdoors. They move laterally to additional systems. They exfiltrate data or deploy ransomware. Each system they reach opens new paths to even more systems.

The entire chain, from initial access to full domain compromise, can take as little as 27 minutes in well-segmented environments, and 27 seconds in poorly managed ones. CrowdStrike measured the average eCrime breakout time at 29 minutes in 2025, with the fastest recorded breakout taking 27 seconds. Meanwhile, the average time to identify and contain a breach is 241 days. The ratio between attacker speed and defender response time is 771,200 to 1.

Why Traditional Security Controls Fail

The reason lateral movement succeeds so consistently is not because of a technology gap. It is because the security architecture of most organizations -- including the vast majority of Trinidad businesses -- was designed for a different threat model.

The perimeter model is dead. For decades, security focused on the network edge. Firewalls blocked unsolicited inbound traffic. VPNs secured remote access. Internal traffic was assumed to be trusted. But attackers who compromise a single account are already inside the perimeter. By the time they start moving laterally, the firewall is irrelevant. The perimeter model assumes the attacker is outside. Lateral movement assumes the attacker is inside.

Endpoint security does not stop internal movement. Antivirus, EDR, and endpoint hardening detect malware and suspicious processes. They do not stop an attacker who is using legitimate administrative tools -- RDP, SSH, PowerShell, SMB -- to move between systems. When the tools being used are the same tools IT administrators use every day, endpoint protection sees normal activity. The attacker is not deploying malware. They are using the exact same protocols and tools that keep your business running. The difference is in who is running them and what they are doing.

Firewall rules are for the edge, not the inside. Most organizations have firewall rules at the perimeter but little or no internal firewalls. East-west traffic -- traffic between systems inside the network -- is unrestricted by default. If you are in the finance VLAN, you can reach the finance server, the HR system, the marketing share, the development environment, and the executive mailbox. This is because flat networks were designed for convenience and accessibility, not security. As Zero Networks noted, organizations have built networks optimized for access and connectivity where containment is an afterthought.

VLANs are not security boundaries. Many organizations believe that dividing their network into VLANs provides security. It does not. VLANs separate broadcast domains but do not enforce access controls between segments. Without additional controls, anyone in the same VLAN can reach anything else in that segment. Attackers who land in a broad segment have room to explore and exploit. VLANs are a networking convenience, not a security control.

Logging is insufficient for detection. Most organizations log inbound traffic at the perimeter but have minimal logging of internal traffic. Security teams cannot contain what they cannot see, and without identity-attributed east-west authentication logs, lateral movement goes undetected until it is too late. Nearly half of all lateral movement incidents go undetected, according to Illumio's 2025 report, primarily because of limited and fragmented visibility across hybrid environments.

The Scale of the Problem in 2026

The data from 2026 reports paints a comprehensive picture of the lateral movement threat landscape:

  • Internal reachability. Zero Networks' 2026 report analyzed 54 trillion network activities across 312 enterprise environments. 80 percent of servers were reachable from anywhere inside the network. 87 percent accepted inbound RDP or SSH from broad internal sources. 78.7 percent were reachable over SMB or WinRM. A single compromised host could reach 85 percent of internal systems on the first hop and effectively 100 percent on the second.
  • Successful breach involvement. The 2025 Illumio Global Cloud Detection and Response Report found that 60 percent of successful breaches involve lateral movement. Nearly 90 percent of organizations experienced a cybersecurity incident involving lateral movement in the past year. Each incident involving lateral movement resulted in a global average of over 7 hours of downtime.
  • Privileged access sprawl. 99 percent of users, roles, and services hold excessive standing permissions, often unused for 60 days or more. Over 80 percent of attacks leverage stolen credentials at some stage. Service accounts now outnumber human identities 109 to 1, yet only 2.6 percent of workload identity permissions are actually used, and 51 percent of workload identities are completely inactive.
  • Legacy authentication. 43.2 percent of observed internal authentication traffic uses NTLM. Microsoft reports that more than 99 percent of password-spray attacks use legacy authentication protocols that bypass multi-factor authentication entirely. Disabling legacy authentication in Entra ID reduces account compromise risk by 67 percent.
  • Alert fatigue. 67 percent of security teams (79 percent in the U.S.) receive more alerts than they can effectively investigate, averaging over 2,000 alerts per day -- one every 42 seconds. 92 percent of organizations have experienced security incidents due to missed or uninvestigated alerts. On average, it takes 12.1 hours to detect an issue caused by a missed alert.
  • Direct user-to-server access. 12.2 percent of organizations demonstrate direct user-to-server administrative pathways, meaning a compromised laptop can directly reach domain controllers, backup systems, ERP platforms, and cloud management consoles without any intermediate hops.

These numbers are not abstract. They describe the actual architecture of networks like the one that would connect a typical Trinidad business: a small IT team managing a handful of servers, a mix of cloud and on-premises systems, remote employees connecting from home, and a network where everyone shares the same VLAN because it is simpler than managing multiple segments. When that network experiences a phishing incident -- and Trinidad reports that 70 percent of all incidents are phishing -- the attacker has immediate access to everything.

AI-Driven Lateral Movement: The 2026 Accelerator

A new dimension to lateral movement is emerging in 2026: artificial intelligence is accelerating the attack chain in ways that compress breach timelines from days to minutes.

Roughly 80 percent of organizations have deployed AI agents in some form, and two-thirds have no governance policies for them. These AI agents hold broad standing access across email, databases, cloud APIs, and code repositories -- standing access that an attacker who compromises or manipulates an agent inherits immediately. The agent's legitimate permissions become the attack path.

AI-powered vulnerability research is also changing the economics of exploitation. Models like Anthropic's Mythos have demonstrated the ability to identify exploitable weaknesses and develop attack paths with limited human involvement, compressing the window between patch release and exploitation beyond what most patch cycles can accommodate. The result is that attackers no longer wait for vulnerability disclosures. They find exploitable internal services autonomously, using AI to scan for vulnerabilities, develop exploits, and move laterally across environments that were designed for connectivity, not containment.

For organizations deploying AI tools -- and most Trinidad businesses that have moved to cloud-based ERP, CRM, or collaboration platforms have some form of AI agent in their stack -- this creates a new class of risk: an AI service account with excessive permissions that no one has audited, no one has monitored, and no one thought to restrict.

How Lateral Movement Affects Trinidad Businesses

The lateral movement threat chain is directly relevant to Trinidad's incident patterns, and the attack patterns seen in the region mirror global trends with some local specifics.

Phishing is the entry point. With 70 percent of incidents being phishing and BEC, every phishing click is a potential lateral movement starting point. A compromised email account can reach every internal system that email is connected to -- file shares, cloud storage, ERP systems, HR databases. Without segmentation, that single compromised email account is a key to everything.

IT teams are small and overworked. Most Trinidad businesses do not have dedicated security operations centers or full-time network security staff. The IT team manages servers, handles help desk tickets, and maintains VPN access. Lateral movement requires active network visibility and response capability that small IT teams simply do not have the bandwidth to provide. By the time they notice unusual internal traffic, the attacker has already moved.

Legacy protocols are still in use. Many Trinidad businesses run on Windows Active Directory environments where NTLM authentication, shared credentials, and broad admin access are the norm. RDP is used freely for remote administration. SMB shares are common. None of these protocols were designed with lateral movement in mind, but they are the exact pathways attackers use to move between systems once they have a foothold.

Cloud adoption creates hybrid visibility gaps. As Trinidad businesses move services to the cloud -- email to Microsoft 365, ERP to the cloud, backups to cloud storage -- the network becomes hybrid by default. But hybrid networks expose the worst of both worlds: on-premises environments that lack cloud-level segmentation controls, and cloud environments that lack visibility into on-premises traffic. The attack surface expands without the visibility to detect lateral movement across the boundary.

The financial impact is concentrated. Trinidad's banking community lost over TT$3 million to cyber-enabled fraud in a single fiscal year, according to the TTPS. While these incidents were primarily BEC and phishing, the same accounts that enable those frauds -- compromised credentials, minimal access controls, flat network access -- are also the accounts that enable lateral movement. If an attacker who compromises a finance employee's credentials can move laterally to reach the ERP database, the financial impact multiplies from a single fraud to a full enterprise compromise.

What Network Segmentation Does

Network segmentation divides your environment into zones with controlled access between them. The goal is simple: limit the blast radius when something goes wrong. If ransomware hits one workstation, segmentation prevents it from encrypting your entire file server estate. If a phishing attack compromises a finance employee's account, segmentation prevents that account from reaching the payroll database, the HR system, and the executive email.

Modern segmentation has evolved through several generations. VLAN-based segmentation tied access to IP addresses and departments. Firewall-based segmentation added perimeters but created rule sprawl that small teams could not maintain. Software-defined segmentation centralized policy management but remained often IP-centric. Identity-based segmentation -- the current standard -- ties access to the identity of the user, device, or application requesting the connection. Instead of asking "is this IP allowed to reach that port," you ask "is this user, on this device, authorized to access this application right now?"

The difference between traditional and identity-based segmentation is critical. A VLAN can separate departments but cannot prevent an attacker in one department from using stolen credentials to access resources in another. An identity-based segmentation policy can, because it checks not just who is connecting but what that identity is authorized to access, what device they are using, and whether the device meets security requirements.

What Trinidad Businesses Should Do

Protecting against lateral movement does not require a complete network redesign. It requires a shift in how access is controlled and monitored. The following steps provide a practical path from flat network to segmented, contained environment.

Close administrative protocols by default. RDP, SMB, SSH, WinRM, and WMI are the primary pathways for lateral movement, accounting for over 70 percent of enterprise threat activity. These protocols should be closed by default -- only opened on demand with per-session authorization and multi-factor authentication. Just-in-time access enforcement minimizes risk without disrupting critical workflows. If no one is actively using RDP from a particular host, the path should be closed. Opening it for a specific session and closing it again after use limits the attack surface to the exact time it is needed.

Enforce least-privilege connectivity. Every communication path between systems should be explicitly justified. Critical systems should be isolated by default, with administrative access tightly scoped and time-bound. The principle is simple: a finance workstation does not need to reach the development environment, the production database, or the backup system. It needs to reach the finance application. Grant access to the finance application. Nothing more.

Implement identity-based segmentation. Tie access policies to user identity, device posture, and application context -- not IP addresses, which change constantly. Users, servers, service accounts, and cloud workloads should be first-class objects in your segmentation policy. If a user's laptop is unpatched or their credentials are compromised, the segmentation layer can deny access regardless of what IP address the device is using.

Isolate the control plane. Active Directory, IAM platforms, backup systems, virtualization hypervisors, Kubernetes API servers, and cloud management consoles are the operational brains of the environment. They should sit in dedicated management zones with restricted admin paths and MFA-gated access. If an attacker reaches your backup infrastructure, recovery operations themselves may fail. If they compromise Active Directory, they can authenticate to everything. These systems must be explicitly isolated from user endpoints and general network traffic.

Retire legacy authentication. NTLM is the canonical example. 43.2 percent of internal authentication still uses it, and it can be exploited by any attacker who obtains a hashed credential. Microsoft reports that more than 99 percent of password-spray attacks use legacy authentication protocols that bypass MFA. Disabling legacy authentication in Entra ID reduces account compromise risk by 67 percent. Every legacy protocol left active is an authentication path that bypasses modern controls.

Deploy east-west visibility. Security teams cannot contain what they cannot see. Most organizations have reasonable perimeter logging but inconsistent logging of internal traffic. Deploy identity-attributed east-west traffic logs tied to specific identities rather than IP addresses. Configure alerting on unusual internal movement -- a workstation connecting to a database server, a service account authenticating from an unusual location, mass internal SMB traffic. Without this visibility, lateral movement goes undetected by design.

Audit service account permissions. Service accounts outnumber human identities 109 to 1 in many environments, yet they are systematically under-monitored. 51 percent of workload identities are completely inactive, carrying permissions that no one remembers granting. An inactive service account with standing admin access is a dormant attack path waiting for an attacker to find it. Scope every service account to the minimum permissions its function requires, enforce regular credential rotation, and monitor for use from unsanctioned sources.

Plan for incremental rollout. Big-bang segmentation projects fail. Start with one business process and two supporting applications. Prove value. Then expand. Your team learns the tooling, your users adapt to new access patterns, and your documentation improves iteratively. The goal is not perfection on day one. The goal is a segmented network that gets progressively more resilient over time.

The Compliance Angle

For Trinidad businesses in regulated sectors, network segmentation is not optional. It is a compliance requirement.

ISO 27001 requires access controls that limit unauthorized access to information assets. SOC 2 requires logical access controls that restrict access to systems based on user roles. PCI DSS requires network segmentation to isolate the cardholder data environment from untrusted networks. Without segmentation, these controls cannot be demonstrated through network architecture. They can only be asserted in a policy document -- and auditors increasingly require evidence that controls are enforced at the network layer, not just documented.

The CARICOM Cyber Security and Cybercrime Action Plan (CCSCAP), launched in October 2025, establishes the framework for harmonized cybercrime legislation and incident response protocols across member states. Organizations that demonstrate proper network segmentation -- documented policies, access reviews, east-west traffic monitoring, and access governance -- show compliance with these frameworks. Organizations that operate flat networks with no evidence of access control enforcement face increasing regulatory risk as regional frameworks mature.
